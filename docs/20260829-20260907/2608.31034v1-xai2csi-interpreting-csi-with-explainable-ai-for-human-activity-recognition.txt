# XAI2CSI: Interpreting CSI with eXplainable AI for Human Activity Recognition 

Idio Guarino ∗, Alfredo Nascita †, Domenico Ciuonzo †, Damiano Carra ‡, Antonio Pescapé †∗University of Bologna, Italy , †University of Napoli Federico II, Italy , ‡University of Verona, Italy ,idio.guarino@unibo.it, damiano.carra@univr.it, {alfredo.nascita, domenico.ciuonzo, pescape}@unina.it, 

Abstract —Wi-Fi Channel State Information (CSI) has emerged as a key enabler for device-free Human Activity Recognition (HAR), enabling low-cost, unobtrusive sensing using existing communication infrastructure. However, Deep Learning (DL) models trained on CSI data often struggle to generalize across users, environments, and device setups due to the context sen-sitivity of wireless propagation. Despite this challenge, limited attention has been devoted to understanding model decisions and generalization failures. This paper introduces XAI2CSI, a frame-work that leverages eXplainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) to analyze DL-based CSI sensing systems. XAI2CSI employs SAGE, a model-agnostic explainability method, to quantify temporal, spectral, and spatial CSI contributions to HAR decisions under nominal and cross-context evaluations on IEEE 802.11ax data. Our analysis reveals that the considered DL model exhibits limited robustness to unseen conditions due to an over-reliance on context-specific CSI patterns, causing models to misinterpret the underlying signal dynamics when deployment conditions change. The proposed methodology and findings provide areference framework to explore alternative solutions and guide the development of robust, transparent Wi-Fi sensing systems. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

In recent years, Deep Learning (DL) has been increasingly adopted to leverage wireless signals for environmental sens-ing [1]. A rich source of information is the Channel State Infor-mation (CSI) from Wi-Fi signals, which has become a key en-abler for device-free Human Activity Recognition (HAR) [2]. By exploiting subtle disturbances induced by human move-ment on radio-frequency propagation, CSI enables low-cost and non-intrusive sensing using existing commercial-off-the-shelf communication hardware, thus avoiding wearable-device limitations such as limited battery life, user discomfort, and complex deployment. Despite its promise, the main obstacle 

to widespread adoption is generalizability : DL models trained on the CSI data may struggle when deployed across different users, environments, or device setups due to wireless prop-agation’s high sensitivity to context [3]. Multipath effects, where signals reflect off walls, furniture, and people, create a unique CSI “fingerprint” for each specific setting. Conse-quently, even minor changes to the physical environment alter this fingerprint, degrading model performance and preventing the development of a universal, “one-size-fits-all” model that can be trained once and deployed anywhere without extensive retraining. Although numerous studies have documented these performance degradations, they largely treat generalization as a performance problem, leaving open a key question: why do CSI-based HAR models fail under unseen conditions ?Answering this question is challenging because DL models operate as black boxes , making it unclear which CSI compo-nents drive successful predictions and how their contribution changes across deployment contexts. eXplainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) provides tools to interpret complex models by identifying the features that most influence their decisions. However, despite the acknowledged challenges in CSI-based sensing, the systematic application of XAI to understand model behavior and generalization remains largely unexplored .This paper bridges this gap through XAI2CSI , a framework that applies XAI to analyze CSI-based HAR under nominal and cross-context conditions. Specifically, XAI2CSI leverages SAGE [4], a novel model-agnostic explainability method, to quantify temporal, spectral, and spatial CSI contributions to HAR decisions. Through rigorous evaluations on IEEE 802.11ax data [5], we investigate the models’ failures in cross-user scenarios by analyzing feature importance across the spatial, temporal, and spectral dimensions of the CSI input. Our analysis reveals limited robustness to unseen conditions due to an over-reliance on context-specific CSI patterns, causing models to misinterpret the underlying signal dynamics when deployment conditions change. These findings provide actionable insights toward robust, transparent, and interpretable Wi-Fi sensing systems. Our contributions are: (i) we evaluate DL-based HAR models under standalone and combined CSI representations (amplitude, phase difference, and their combination) using 802.11ax hardware; (ii ) we perform systematic cross-domain evaluations across users, environments, and receiver node positions to assess model generalization; ( iii ) we apply SAGE to provide a compre-hensive XAI analysis, characterizing temporal, spectral, and spatial CSI contributions under distribution shifts. The paper is organized as follows: Sec. II reviews related works. Sec. III details our XAI methodology. Secs. IV and V present the setup and results, while Sec. VI concludes it. II. R ELATED WORKS

A key limitation is the lack of native CSI extraction on com-modity devices [19]. Early works relied on specialized tools such as the Linux 802.11n CSI Tool , thus constraining research to outdated 802.11n hardware. Although newer frameworks— e.g., Nexmon for 802.11ac and AX-CSI for 802.11ax—enable richer data collection, many recent works still rely on legacy tools, thereby limiting their relevance to modern Wi-Fi deploy-ment [19]. Only a few studies leverage the wider bandwidths supported by modern Wi-Fi standards [5, 16]. Most HAR approaches rely on CSI amplitude for its simplicity and real-time suitability [1]. Alternatives such as phase differences, Doppler spectrum, short-time Fourier transform, or body ve-locity estimates capture richer motion information but increase computational cost, require calibration, and may be affected by orientation and multipath distortions [1]. A major challenge across these works is generalization: CSI strongly depends on users, environments, and hardware, often causing severe performance drops when models are tested across different settings [2]. Although this problem is acknowledged, several studies still evaluate their models in the same context in which they were trained [6, 7, 11, 13, 15, 18]. In contrast, other works explicitly assess generalization by testing models on users, environments, or device placements that are not seen during training [5, 8, 9, 10, 12, 14, 16, 17]. To address these challenges, prior works have explored two main strategies. The first involves signal preprocessing to isolate motion-related components and filter out environment-dependent multipath effects [14, 16]. While improving stability, these methods may also discard valuable cues together with noise. The second uses learning-based adaptation strategies, e.g., transfer-learning and few-shot learning [9, 17], which improve cross-domain performance but require additional data collection in each new context, limiting real-world deployability. Recent evaluations further show that, despite these efforts, generaliza-tion across unseen domains remains limited [5]. Finally, while DL-based approaches become the dominant paradigm, they typically behave as black boxes, offering little insight into their decision-making process. To the best of our knowledge, no prior works have explored explainability beyond performance metrics. Our work fills this gap by leveraging SAGE to systematically analyze model behavior, thus uncovering the intrinsic mechanisms that govern decision-making and paving the way for more transparent Wi-Fi sensing systems. 

Positioning. Our work advances prior research in three directions : (i) evaluating HAR models under standalone and combined CSI representations (amplitude, phase differ-ence, and their integration via “early" fusion) using modern Wi-Fi 802.11ax hardware; ( ii ) performing systematic cross-do-main evaluations across users, environments, and receiver node locations to assess model generalization; and ( iii ) applying SAGE (the core of XAI2CSI) for a comprehensive XAI anal-ysis, providing a global view of model decision mechanisms. III. M ETHODOLOGY 

CSI background. CSI characterizes the signal propagation be-tween transmitter and receiver, encapsulating reflection, scat-tering, diffraction, and multipath effects. It supports commu-nication optimization by enabling tasks such as beamforming and rate adaptation [1]. In an Orthogonal Frequency Division Multiplexing (OFDM) system such as Wi-Fi, CSI provides a detailed estimate of the wireless channel in the frequency domain. Thanks to the wide bandwidth of Wi-Fi signals and the fine granularity of OFDM subcarriers, this information is valuable for both communication optimization and device-free sensing applications, e.g., indoor localization, and human activity monitoring. Formally, in an OFDM system with F

subcarriers and a single spatial stream, the channel can be modeled in the frequency domain as r = h ⊗ s + n, where 

r ∈ CF is the received OFDM symbol, h ∈ CF is the channel frequency response (i.e., the CSI), s ∈ CF is the transmitted OFDM symbol, n ∈ CF is the noise vector, and ⊗ denotes the Hadamard product. The channel response h captures the amplitude attenuation and phase shift of each subcarrier. The receiver can estimate the channel ( c ≃ h) by computing the ratio between the received r and transmitted s signals. In Multiple-Input Multiple-Output (MIMO) systems with 

Ntx transmit antennas and Nrx receive antennas, the CSI at time t can be represented as a tensor H(t) ∈ CNtx ×Nrx ×F,which captures the channel response for every transmit–receive antenna pair across all subcarriers. Specifically, its amplitude and phase components are defined as: A(t) = || H(t)|| and 

ϕ(t) = ̸ H(t), respectively, where A(t), ϕ(t) ∈ RNtx ×Nrx ×F.CSI is collected by capturing Wi-Fi packets, reflecting both static factors (e.g., room layout) and dynamic ones (e.g., human presence and movement). While primarily used in transceivers for tasks like equalization and beamforming, CSI can also be extracted via dedicated tools. These tools—for each Tx-Rx antenna pair—record the channel estimate across all available subcarriers at a given sampling rate Sx, producing a CSI time series that reflects wireless channel evolution.

Pre-processing. Following [5], we apply a series of trace-level processing steps, before feeding the CSI data into the DL model: ( i) CSI data is downsampled to 80MHz channels with F = 1024 subcarriers by selecting central frequency bins, exploiting the redundancy in high-resolution CSI and reducing computational load, and non-informative subcarri-ers are discarded using a predefined filter ( F: 1024 →994). (ii ) Values are normalized per receiver antenna by their mean amplitude. ( iii ) For amplitude features, a Hampel filter [19] is applied along the time dimension to mitigate outliers. ( iv ) The time series is segmented into overlapping windows of W = 50 packets ( ∆ ≈ 333ms ) with a stride of 25 (i.e., 50% overlap). Finally, Min–Max scaling is applied to the receiver antenna, with parameters computed solely from the training set. 

Model Considered. We employ a 2D-CNN consisting of three 2D convolutional layers with kernel sizes 7, (5 , 3) , and (3 , 3) 

and strides (3 , 1) , (2 , 2) , and 1, respectively. Each layer has ReLU activation and is followed by a 2×2 max-pooling layer. The output of the convolutional block is flattened and passed through a dense layer with 128 units and ReLU activation, followed by a softmax-activated dense layer for classification with C units. To regularize training and reduce overfitting, a dropout (rate of 0.3) is applied after both the last pooling and the first dense layer. The frame ¯Xi is structured as a 

(D, F, W) tensor, treating D as the spatial-feature dimension. This arrangement enables the 2D-CNN to capture joint correla-tions across antennas, subcarriers, and time, while facilitating the interpretability of individual components. Following [13], we adopt this architecture for its optimal accuracy-complexity trade-off compared to more complex models (e.g., ResNets, ViT), ensuring real-time execution on edge devices. 

Evaluation Procedure. To ensure reproducible evaluation while preserving causal relationships, each trace is split along the time axis into training and test with an 80 /20% ratio. We further reversed 20% of the training set for validation. Model training was performed for up to 200 epochs using the SGD optimizer. To compute SAGE values, each frame is flattened (D = {1, . . . , D × F × W}) and conditional expectations are approximated using a background of 40 training samples/class with a convergence threshold of 0.05 . This preserves the orig-inal frame structure, enabling the grouping of features by sub-carrier, antenna, or time index for interpretable analysis. VI. D ISCUSSION AND CONCLUSIONS 

This paper presented XAI2CSI, a framework embedding SAGE as its core to systematically apply XAI to CSI-based HAR and investigate the behavior of DL models under realistic conditions. Using different CSI input representations (amplitude, phase difference, and their combination) collected from modern IEEE 802.11ax hardware, we first evaluated model generalization to unseen users, environments, and re-ceiver positions, showing that performance drops significantly when the deployment context changes.
